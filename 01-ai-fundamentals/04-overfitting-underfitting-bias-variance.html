Overfitting, Underfitting, Bias & Variance

Learn generalization, read training and validation trends, and understand how to reduce overfitting.

1. Generalization

Generalization is a model's ability to perform well on representative data it has not seen during training. Memorizing training examples is not the goal; learning reusable patterns is.

2. Underfitting vs good fit vs overfitting

CaseTraining performanceValidation performanceInterpretation
UnderfittingPoorPoorModel has not learned enough useful pattern.
Good fitGoodGoodUseful patterns generalize.
OverfittingExcellentPoorModel learned training-specific noise or quirks.

When training loss keeps falling while validation loss starts rising, overfitting may be occurring. Select checkpoints using validation performance, not just the final training loss.

3. Bias and variance

High bias

Model assumptions are too simplistic; commonly associated with underfitting. Example: predicting failure from CPU alone.

High variance

Model is too sensitive to the training sample; commonly associated with overfitting. Example: learning cluster-specific noise.

Balanced

Captures useful patterns that remain effective on new representative data.

The word “bias” here describes a statistical error pattern. It is different from the learned bias parameter in a neuron's equation.

4. Ways to reduce overfitting

  • Regularization: penalize unnecessarily complex solutions (for example, L1 or L2).
  • Early stopping: stop when validation performance stops improving and keep the best checkpoint.
  • Representative data: include varied clusters, workloads, traffic and node types.
  • Feature selection: remove irrelevant, unstable or leakage-prone features.
  • Reduce complexity: choose a model appropriate to the task and data.

5. MLOps perspective

Track dataset and code versions, training/validation metrics, checkpoints, resource consumption and evaluation gates. Do not promote a model based only on training accuracy.
